02 / HOW WE GOT HERE

Build. Walk. Look closer. Adjust.

The direction stayed steady: arrive by a path, see the home before reaching it, and find a welcome at the threshold. Each pass answered a different question.

01

SEPTEMBER 12 · THE FIRST WORLD

Give the journey a shape.

We began with a coastal trail, silver grass, dark conifers, pale circular masonry, and a brass-ribbed dome. Lanterns made a line the eye could follow. The architecture was visible in the distance, so walking had a destination.

The first preserved version already had a complete arrival: continuous terrain, a deeply seated foundation, a door that opened, procedural coastal sound, and Astra’s welcome.

What carried forward

Establish the route, scale, and recognizable silhouette early. The later material work could then enrich a place that already made sense.

Walk the original release ↗
02

SEPTEMBER 12 · REFINING THE FIRST RELEASE

Make the walk hold together.

Christopher’s Aether Grove trials supplied the important bodily lessons: a continuous floor, balanced trail boundaries, and smooth turning. Stillwater adopted gaze-relative walking, 58°/second continuous turning, a reachable threshold, and an in-world greeting.

Inspection caught an inner dome projecting through the outer roof. The door needed to swing inward. Static geometry was combined by material, reducing an early approach measurement from 156 draw calls to 35.

What we learned

Comfort and visual continuity belong in the foundation of the scene. Small geometry errors become very conspicuous at human scale.

These refinements are included in V1. Separate snapshots of the earlier working states were not retained.

03

SEPTEMBER 12–13 · THE MATERIAL PASS

Give each surface a character.

Christopher asked for more realism in the grass, stones, trees, lights, and observatory. Scanned gravel, worn paving, bark, moss-rock surfaces, and plaster supplied the irregular detail that the first version lacked.

Three small scanned rock shapes replaced the repeated faceted boulder. Photographic grass tufts and needle sprigs gave the vegetation finer edges. Framed lanterns and a small number of nearby lights helped connect illumination to the surfaces around it.

What we learned

Realism comes from several scales working together: the outline of a rock, the structure of a plant, and the way a small surface catches the light.

04

SEPTEMBER 13 · THE CLOSE-UP PASS

Let the view from the door argue back.

The first texture application stretched a small pattern across large surfaces. We adjusted the scale around the observatory, refined the foliage cutouts, and gave the lantern glazing a frame.

At the threshold, the circular floor revealed another mistake: stripes caused by texture coordinates intended for a cylinder’s side. Mapping the horizontal caps separately restored the surface. The same close-up also confirmed that the opened door left the greeting readable.

What we learned

Inspect the place from where someone will actually stand. A distant composition, a trail-level view, and a close-up reveal different problems.

The image shows the corrected V2 release. The intermediate stretched-texture views were not saved as separate archive files.

04 / FOR THE NEXT PASS

Keep the evidence
while the world changes.

This journal gives us a way to return to decisions, compare them, and choose what to try next.

  1. Save the starting point. Freeze the build before making a new pass.
  2. Name the visible problem. Record the view or moment that needs attention.
  3. Make a focused change. Keep enough context to understand its effect.
  4. Return to the same viewpoints. Capture the trailhead, approach, courtyard, and door.
  5. Archive the result and the lesson. Preserve the source, images, relevant checks, and what remains unresolved.

The next change can begin with a comparison, rather than a guess.
